01 · The biology

What DMD is

DMD is a genetic condition caused by changes in the dystrophin gene. The protein it encodes helps protect muscle fibers from damage during contraction.

What dystrophin normally does

Dystrophin acts like a structural link inside a muscle cell, helping connect the cell's internal framework to supporting proteins at the cell membrane. This helps stabilize the membrane when a muscle contracts.

Conceptual illustration, not to scale. Dystrophin is one part of a larger protein complex.

What happens in DMD

When functional dystrophin is missing or severely disrupted, muscle fibers are more vulnerable to injury as they contract and relax. Repeated damage contributes to progressive muscle weakness and the replacement of muscle tissue with fat and connective tissue.

The key idea: DMD is not simply a problem with muscles being “weak.” It is a problem with the protection and maintenance of muscle fibers over time.
02 · My genetic variant

My exon 45–50 deletion

My DMD gene is missing exons 45 through 50. This deletion disrupts the full-length form of dystrophin that skeletal and heart muscle depend on.

Why this matters for muscle

Dp427 is the full-length dystrophin made in skeletal and heart muscle. Because the deletion shifts the gene's reading frame, my muscle cells can't produce working dystrophin. That leads to the gradual muscle damage described above, and it's also why DMD affects the heart.

03 · Physical effects

How DMD affects my body

The biology is important, but the day-to-day experience is more personal. These are the ways the condition shows up in my life.

Mobility and strength

When I was about 6 to 8 years old, I started walking on my toes and getting up off the floor in a different way after falling. On a trip to India (my last trip to India, in 2009), a doctor noticed how I was walking and said we should test for muscular dystrophy. We did, and I was diagnosed around age 9.

Over time, walking long distances and climbing stairs slowly got harder, but I was still able to play soccer and do karate for a while. By 5th and 6th grade (ages 10 to 11), I needed a wheelchair for long distances. Eventually I couldn't stand at all, and in 7th grade (around ages 12 to 13), I started using a power wheelchair full-time.

At this point, I can still move my lower legs somewhat, but I can't lift my legs up. I can move my arms and hands fairly well, but I have limited ability to raise my arms.

I swam regularly until I was about 19, since the water helped support my weight and made it easier to move. Now, getting into and out of pools has become difficult enough that I no longer swim.

Heart and breathing

It's also affected my heart function. A typical healthy ejection fraction is around 55–70%, while mine is currently around 35%. My lung capacity is reduced too, although it isn't very noticeable in everyday conversation at this stage. I sometimes need to take breaths a little more frequently when speaking. Reduced respiratory strength also makes it harder to project my voice, so I sometimes have to speak louder to compensate.

04 · Family, accessibility, and independence

How DMD shapes my day-to-day life

⌂

Family and caregiving

My parents have been my primary caregivers throughout my life. My whole family moved to Southern California in 2019 so they could support me while I was at Caltech. Since returning to the Bay Area, we've hired caregivers too, but my parents remain my main support system. These days I usually need special equipment to lift me, and I need help with a lot of daily tasks, especially my morning and bedtime routines, hygiene, and using the restroom. My parents and caregivers share that help. Most of these tasks are easier with two people, though they're possible with one, so our caregivers are mainly there as an extra pair of hands so that both of my parents don't always have to help.

Because my morning routine takes longer than most people's, I usually wake up early and go to bed on time (except when there's something late on the weekend). It has become second nature.

After my PhD, I'd like to live more independently with a caregiver.

♿

Social activities and accessibility

It also affects how I plan social activities. Even going to a restaurant takes some planning to make sure it's wheelchair accessible, with enough room to maneuver, tables at the right height, and accessible restrooms. Concerts and other events take planning too. I have to consider things like wheelchair-accessible seating, how easily I can get around the venue, and whether there are accessible restrooms. We have an accessible car, so getting around locally is manageable.

I still enjoy going out to concerts and movies with friends, and those are generally manageable since they're usually not too far away and don't require too much planning. But I'm also quite comfortable keeping myself busy at home. I enjoy watching movies and YouTube, reading, and doing things indoors. Sometimes I'd rather hang out with friends locally or at home and do something indoors. Going out can take more effort because of all the accessibility planning, so it sometimes comes down to how interested I am in doing something versus how much effort it'll take to arrange.

Accessible on paper isn't always accessible to me. A building can meet accessibility requirements and still be hard for me to get around in. Because my arms are weak and I can't raise them very far, I may not be able to reach the push button that opens an automatic door if it's mounted too high. Elevators can have the same problem. A building having an elevator doesn't help much if I can't reach the buttons to call it or pick a floor. Details like these are part of why I check places ahead of time rather than assuming that "accessible" means accessible for me.
↗

Travel

Travel is a little more complicated. I generally haven't flown since I started using a power wheelchair full-time. You have to transfer out of your wheelchair and have it transported in the cargo hold, which creates a risk of damage. My family and I haven't really wanted to take that risk.

Air travel was still possible in theory until I started using a power wheelchair full-time at 13. But my grandparents and my parents' cousins would visit us from India, so we never really needed to travel there. I haven't been to India since 2009, but we've still been able to see each other over the years. And now, with WhatsApp and video calls, the distance feels much smaller.

Road trips are possible since I can travel in our accessible car, but we have to plan around accessible hotels and restroom stops. We've driven to LA a few times. Driving within California is generally more feasible, but it still requires planning.

Overall, I don't feel that I'm missing out by not traveling much. I'm happy spending time with friends locally.

✋

Staying as independent as possible

In general, I try to be as independent as possible and use my own muscles whenever I can, which helps preserve strength and flexibility. For example, I often sing and play the piano and harmonica. They're hobbies I genuinely enjoy, and they're also good exercise for my lungs and fine motor control, helping me preserve that function for as long as possible.

How it shapes me
Because of all these experiences, I've become someone who is empathetic, plans ahead, stays organized, and pays attention to details. In a way, DMD has made me better at these things, and they're skills that help me well beyond managing my condition.

How it shapes me

I don't usually spend much time thinking about my condition. It is a part of life that I've learned to work around, not something that defines everything about me.

DMD hasn't affected my intelligence or my personality, so unfortunately I can't blame any of my personality traits on it 😂. And for better or worse, I have all the same emotions and feelings as anyone else. Stephen Hawking once said that even though he couldn't move and had to speak through a computer, "in my mind I am free." I feel the same way.

05.1 · Exploring the world

Geography, history, and exploring the world

Some of my interests have also given me ways to connect with places and experiences even when I haven't been there physically.

A long-standing interest in geography and history

I've always been really interested in geography and history. In middle school, I even competed in the California State Geography Bee. I've often learned about places in so much detail that I can recognize or talk about them even if I haven't actually traveled there. Sometimes people are surprised by how much I know about a place I've never visited.

Traveling virtually

In a way, I've traveled virtually through atlases, maps, videos, and other ways of exploring the world. So when my friends talk about their travels, I can often relate to the places they're describing, even if I haven't been there myself. I also play GeoGuessr now, although my geography skills have gotten a bit rusty without regular practice.

06 · Perspective

How I think about it

“I genuinely don't feel bad about it; it's just the reality of my life. But I do think it's important for people to understand what it actually involves, rather than making assumptions based on what they see.”

DMD shapes some of the logistics of my life and has real physical and medical consequences. At the same time, it doesn't tell you what I enjoy, what I value, what I can contribute, or what kind of life I want to build.
